# Facts 01 - Survival video idea verification (researched 2026-11-01)

Note: Several official pages (readyforwildfire.org, fire.ca.gov, Wikipedia-adjacent) returned HTTP 403 or were only visible via search snippets; those items are marked accordingly. Metric units used; US-audience figures in inches kept where the source uses them.

## B. ICE

**B1. The 1-10-1 principle**
- Fact: Giesbrecht's mnemonic: ~1 min to get breathing under control (cold shock), ~10 min of meaningful muscle use (cold incapacitation), ~1 hour before hypothermia causes unconsciousness.
- Number: 1 / 10 / 1 (minutes / minutes / hour).
- Source: https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/article/medical-did-you-know/professor-popsicles-physiological-proof ; https://oldprod.outdoors.org/?p=6358 (seen via search snippets, not fully fetched)
- Confidence: high that the principle is attributed to Giesbrecht and is stated this way; med on exact timings.
- Correction/nuance: It is a simplified guide, not a timetable. Hypothermia takes at least ~30 min to set in; "1 hour" is roughly time to unconsciousness in near-freezing water and varies with body fat, clothing, water temp. Most deaths occur in the first two phases (cold shock / swim failure), not hypothermia. Say "about", never "exactly".
- Safety: OK to present as awareness. Don't promise a guaranteed hour.

**B2. Minnesota DNR ice thickness**
- Fact: DNR table is for NEW, CLEAR ice only.
- Current DNR numbers (fetched): under 4 in (10 cm) stay off; 4 in (10 cm) on foot; 5-7 in (13-18 cm) snowmobile / small ATV; 7-8 in (18-20 cm) side-by-side; 9-10 in (23-25 cm) small car or SUV; 11-12 in (28-30 cm) medium SUV / small truck; 13 in (33 cm) medium truck; 16-17 in (40-43 cm) heavy-duty truck.
- Source: https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/safety/ice/thickness.html
- Confidence: high.
- CORRECTION: The claim "8-12 in car, 12-15 in truck" is OUTDATED/off. Current DNR says 9-10 in small car/SUV, 11-12 in medium SUV/small truck, 13 in medium truck. Older versions of the DNR/other-agency tables used 8-12 in car and 12-15 in truck. Use the current figures.
- Caveats to show on screen: white (snow-ice) is about half as strong, so double the thickness; thickness varies across a lake; DNR does not measure ice, you must check; current/springs/sun weaken ice.
- Safety: Never imply "4 in is safe". Say "minimum for one person on foot, on new clear ice, and only after checking". Strongly flag: do not drive on ice as a video takeaway.

**B3. Self-rescue steps**
- Fact: Turn back toward the direction you came from (likely strongest ice), kick horizontally while pushing chest onto ice, then roll (or wriggle) away from the hole rather than standing.
- Source: no DNR page found with steps; supporting: https://www.redcross.ca/blog/2019/1/what-you-need-to-know-if-you-fall-through-the-ice ; https://www.police1.com/officer-safety/articles/you-just-fell-through-ice-9-ways-to-stay-alive-eEhG2RQcSbXscwph/ ; https://georgianbay.ca/self-rescue-what-to-do-if-you-fall-through-ice (snippets)
- Confidence: med-high (consistent across rescue instructors; not verified against a single official US page). Recommend verifying on the Minnesota DNR "If you fall in" page before publishing.
- Add: stay calm, get breathing under control first (ties to 1-10-1), ice picks help, once out get warm and seek medical care (after-drop/hypothermia).
- Safety: Don't present as a reliable trick; prevention beats rescue. Fine as awareness.

## A. TORNADO

**A1. Overpasses are not safe shelter**
- Fact: NWS Norman states highway overpasses are not acceptable storm shelter areas; safety there is "merely an illusion"; overpasses can funnel winds/debris and you become a stationary target.
- Source: https://www.weather.gov/oun/safety-overpass
- Confidence: high.

**A2. 3 deaths near/under overpasses on 3 May 1999**
- Fact: "3 people killed near or underneath highway overpass bridges" on May 3, 1999. Two in the OKC metro (F4/F5 tornado), one in rural Payne County (F2).
- Source: https://www.weather.gov/oun/safety-overpass
- Confidence: high for NWS wording. Nuance: "near or underneath", not strictly "under". Also, many people survived under overpasses that day; the stat is not a death rate.

**A3. 26 April 1991 Andover, Kansas Turnpike video**
- Fact: News crew filmed from a Kansas Turnpike overpass as a tornado passed on 26 Apr 1991; the video spread widely and fueled the myth.
- Source: same NWS page (mentions video from southern Kansas, 26 Apr 1991, Kansas Turnpike overpass; does not name Andover). Andover naming and the F5 / 17 deaths in the Andover community: search results only; verify with NWS Wichita.
- Confidence: high on date/location of video; med on "Andover" naming. Nuance: NWS argues the crew survived because of the unusual overpass construction, the tornado's track and weak intensity there, not because overpasses are safe.

**A4. In a vehicle**
- Fact: NWS Norman guidance: do not try to outrun a tornado; the old advice to drive at right angles to the tornado path is still good when road conditions allow; vehicles are not safe to ride out a tornado.
- NWS/Red Cross 2009 joint statement (via search): get into a sturdy building; if none, options are staying buckled in the vehicle with head below windows covered, or lying flat in a low ditch away from the vehicle; do not shelter under an overpass.
- Sources: https://www.weather.gov/oun/safety-overpass ; https://www.weather.gov/media/safety/NWS-ARC-Revised-Joint-Statement-on-Tornado-Safety_062209.pdf (not fully fetched); https://www.weather.gov/media/ict/handouts/WeatherSafety.pdf
- Confidence: med. The current weather.gov tornado page was not retrieved. Guidance differs slightly by office and age ("get out" vs "stay buckled"). Verify live page before scripting a step list.
- Safety flag: Do not give a simple "do X in a car" instruction as certain; present as "last resort, no good option, get to a sturdy building".

## C. TSUNAMI

**C1. Tilly Smith**
- Fact: Tilly Smith, age 10, at Maikhao (Mai Khao) Beach, Phuket, 26 Dec 2004, recognised the receding, bubbling sea from a geography lesson (teacher Andrew Kearney, Danes Hill School) given about two weeks before, and persuaded her parents; the beach was cleared.
- Number: age 10; "about 100" people credited; ~2 weeks before the lesson. Date 26 Dec 2004.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilly_Smith ; https://www.undrr.org/quick/9021 ; https://www.foxnews.com/story/schoolgirl-saves-nearly-100-lives.amp
- Confidence: high on core story; med on "~100" (press estimate; say "an estimated 100" or "nearly 100"). Maikhao is reported as one of the beaches with no deaths. Some outlets say age 11 (minor outlier). Wave height "9 m" appears in some accounts, unverified.
- Safety: Positive; teaches recognising natural warning signs (rapid sea withdrawal). Good.

**C2. Oregon guidance**
- Fact: Oregon (DOGAMI/OEM-linked materials): a local (Cascadia) tsunami can arrive in roughly 10-20 minutes; materials commonly say 15-20; the shaking is the warning; if shaking is strong/long, evacuate immediately on foot to high ground, no waiting for an official alert; roads may be damaged. Guide suggests at least ~100 ft (30 m) above sea level or beyond the evacuation zone.
- Sources: https://apps.oregon.gov/oregon-newsroom/OR/DOGAMI/Posts/Post/tsunami-awareness-week-is-march-24-to-30--be-prepared--61333 ; https://www.oregon.gov/OEM/Documents/Guest_Service_Directory_2p.pdf ; https://pubs.oregon.gov/dogami/nr/ (press releases)
- Confidence: med. Figures vary (10-20 vs 15-20 min) by location. The exact phrase "strong shaking means go" was not confirmed verbatim; official language is closer to "long or strong shaking: go to high ground". Do not quote as a slogan from the state.
- Safety: Good; always point to the viewer's local evacuation map.

**C3. Waves come in a series; first not necessarily largest**
- Fact: NWS tsunami bulletins state the first wave may not be the largest, tsunamis are a series of waves dangerous for many hours, and each wave can last 5-45 min.
- Source: https://www.tsunami.gov/events/PAAQ/2022/01/15/r5qho6/3/WEAK51/WEAK51TEX.xml ; research example Okal & Synolakis 2016 (max amplitude on 4th wave, 72 min after first, Tohoku at Papeete).
- Confidence: high.

## E. WILDFIRE

**E1. CAL FIRE vehicle entrapment**
- Fact: Ready for Wildfire "What to do if trapped" page (in-vehicle steps) per search snippet: "Park your vehicle in an area clear of vegetation. Close all vehicle windows and vents. Cover yourself with wool blanket or jacket. Lie on vehicle floor." plus call 911.
- URL: https://www.readyforwildfire.org/prepare-for-wildfire/go-evacuation-guide/what-to-do-if-trapped/ (older: http://www.readyforwildfire.org/What-To-Do-If-Trapped/)
- WORDING NOT VERIFIED VERBATIM: site returned HTTP 403 to my fetch. Above is from a search snippet only, so treat it as paraphrase-level. Someone must open the page in a browser and copy the exact text.
- "Gas tanks rarely explode": NOT confirmed on the CAL FIRE page. Seen in other sources: Utah State University Extension ("Metal gas tanks and containers rarely explode"), UC ANR-related guidance. https://extension.usu.edu/preparedness/files/uploads/fire-newsarticle.pdf
- UC ANR Fire Network (fetched, verbatim): "Stay inside the vehicle until the fire has passed." / "The car should remain on with windows rolled up and AC recirculation on." / "Lie on vehicle floor and cover yourself with a natural fibered blanket or jacket." / "Be aware that metal parts of the vehicle will be hot. Avoid direct contact if possible." / "Evacuate on foot as a last resort." https://ucanr.edu/program/uc-anr-fire-network/what-do-when-trapped-wildfire
- Confidence: med (CAL FIRE) / high (UC ANR text).
- Nuance: Sources differ on AC and engine (CAL FIRE says windows/vents closed; UC ANR says keep the car on with recirculating AC; other guidance says engine may stall). Staying in a car is an absolute last resort; leaving early on evacuation orders is the real advice; entrapment in vehicles is a leading cause of wildfire deaths.
- Safety flag (HIGH): Highest risk of being read as "how to survive a wildfire in a car". Must frame as a last resort, with "leave early" as the main message, and do not stage as a stunt or promise survival.

## D. ELEVATOR

**D1. Otis, Crystal Palace, 1854**
- Fact: Elisha Otis demonstrated his safety brake at the Crystal Palace, New York (American Institute / World's Fair), riding a raised platform while the hoist rope was cut; the platform dropped only a few inches.
- Number: 1854 (commonly cited as 24 or 30 May 1854; sources differ).
- Sources: https://www.britannica.com/topic/Elisha-Otis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha_Otis ; https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/72451-first-safety-elevator-lift ; https://theelevatormuseum.org/otis.php
- Confidence: high on event/year; low-med on exact date, on the famous "All safe, gentlemen" quote (not confirmed in my search), and on P.T. Barnum's role (reported but not primary-sourced). Knife vs axe is disputed. Otis was not the inventor of elevators, only of the safety brake.

**D2. Betty Lou Oliver, 1945**
- Fact: After a B-25 hit the Empire State Building on 28 July 1945, elevator operator Betty Lou Oliver survived a fall in an elevator, injured; Guinness lists it as the longest fall survived in an elevator.
- Number: ~75 floors, ~300 m (1,000 ft) (Guinness). Starting floor 79 or 80 in different accounts.
- Sources: https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/73541-longest-fall-survived-in-a-lift-elevator ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1945_Empire_State_Building_B-25_crash
- Confidence: high on the event; med on "75 floors" (the safety brakes were reportedly disabled/cables severed; cushioning by stacked cables, compressed air, and bottom buffers is speculation).
- Safety: Not a survival tip. Do not say "lie on the floor to survive". Use only as history.

**D3. Modern elevators and stuck-elevator advice**
- Fact: Traction elevators have governor-triggered safety gear that grips guide rails; code requires free-fall/overspeed protection; true free-fall is not credible. Stuck passengers should stay inside, use the alarm/call button, and wait.
- Sources: https://www.tkelevator.com/us-en/tools/classroom-on-demand/governor-module.html ; https://patents.google.com/patent/US11897728B2 ; https://www.elevatoruptime.com/guides/stuck-in-elevator
- Confidence: med. These are industry pages, not government. "Cannot free-fall" should be softened to "is designed so it practically cannot; multiple independent brakes". Look for an authoritative source (e.g., ASME A17.1, a state elevator safety office, or CPSC) before filming.
- Safety: Never advise prying doors or climbing out; correct and safe as stated.

## RANKING (strongest, safest, best-sourced reveal)

1. **C. Tsunami (Tilly Smith)**: best-documented, positive, actionable (recognise the sea receding, go high, don't wait for a siren), low risk, well-sourced to UNDRR/Wikipedia/news; supplement with NWS bulletin text for "first wave is not the largest". Only soft spot: "~100 saved" is an estimate.
2. **A. Tornado overpass myth**: NWS Norman primary source (exact slide quotes), a clear myth-vs-fact reveal with a famous video origin, and a safe message (leave the overpass; sturdy building). Caveat: vehicle advice varies by office, so keep the take-home to "an overpass is the worst choice" and "get to a sturdy building"; verify the Andover naming.

Runners-up: B Ice (excellent hook with 1-10-1, but update the DNR thickness table and verify rescue steps on an official page); D Elevator (fun history, weak official sourcing for the modern-safety claims); E Wildfire (highest safety risk; CAL FIRE verbatim not verified).
