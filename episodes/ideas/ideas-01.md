# Ideas 01: five survival-short variants (stage 1, IDEA)

Format: vertical "what to do if…" survival short, 65-90 s, English, US audience, metric units.
Sources read: story-explainer-2d SKILL + concepts-and-hooks, survival-format, premise-lists;
survival-short SKILL + story.md. Excluded on purpose: everything from "14 Minutes" (nuclear alert,
Times Square / NYC street, basement fallout shelter, phone-alert opening shot, food-counting rule).

Every "fact to verify" below is a named, checkable claim for the researcher. Nothing here is
invented. Where I'm unsure of exact wording or numbers I say so.

## Variant A: "Under the Bridge" (tornado x Oklahoma overpass)

- **Premise (WHO + CONSTRAINT + CLOCK + RESOURCE):** You, driving north on I-35 outside Oklahoma
  City on a May afternoon. A tornado has touched down behind you. Ahead, the overpass is packed
  with stopped cars and people climbing up under the bridge deck. You have **about 5 minutes**, a
  car with half a tank, and an open wheat field with a roadside ditch.
- **Twist:** Everyone stops under the overpass because it looks like a concrete roof. You should
  never do that. The narrow gap squeezes the wind into a tunnel, and the embankment lifts you up
  into faster wind and flying debris. You should drive at right angles out of the tornado's path
  if you still can. If you can't, use one of NWS's two last resorts: stay in the car with your
  seatbelt on and your head below the windows, or get lower than the road and lie flat with your
  hands over your head.
- **Hook:** "Tornado behind you on the highway? Never stop under the overpass." (11 words)
  - Alt: "Everyone hides under the overpass. That's the deadliest spot." (9)
- **Persistent world:** A dead-flat Oklahoma interstate under a green-grey sky. A concrete
  overpass with cars parked beneath it, a wheat field, a grain elevator on the horizon, a roadside
  ditch, and the funnel growing in the rear-view mirror. The camera goes car interior, then aerial,
  then ditch level.
- **Diagrams (3 + 1):**
  1. Overpass cross-section: wind arrows bunch up and speed through the gap, and people sit high
     on the embankment slope, right in the fastest part ("WIND TUNNEL").
  2. Top-down map: the tornado's track as an arrow and your car turning 90° onto a side road.
     A label reads "MOVE SIDEWAYS, NOT AHEAD".
  3. Road cross-section: the car on the road compared with the ditch below road level. The hero
     lies flat, hands over head, and debris arrows pass overhead.
  4. (Bonus) A newspaper-clipping card: "1999: 3 DIED UNDER OVERPASSES".
- **HUD:** Distance to tornado (km, ticking down), clock "MAY 3 · 18:52"-style, a 5:00 countdown,
  and a "SHELTER" status chip that changes from CAR to OVERPASS ✗ to DITCH.
- **Cliffhanger / Part 2:** The roar passes and it goes quiet. The hero climbs out of the ditch.
  The car is still there, but the overpass is empty. "The family with the little girl who waved
  at me. Where did they go?" PART 2.
- **Fact to verify (the reveal):** NWS Norman (Oklahoma) says that in the **May 3, 1999** Oklahoma
  tornado outbreak, **three people were killed while sheltering under highway overpasses**. Also
  check the origin of the myth: the **April 26, 1991** TV news crew video filmed under a Kansas
  Turnpike overpass near Andover, Kansas, which made overpasses look safe. Verify the exact death
  count and the NWS wording of the two in-vehicle last-resort options.
- **Main risk:** Safety nuance. NWS says no option in a car is truly safe, so the script must call
  the ditch and the car "last resorts" and never "safe". On originality, weather services have run
  this PSA, but it's rare in the animated survival format.
- **Score: 9/9** (a3 b3 c3)

## Variant B: "Ten Minutes Under" (falling through ice x Minnesota lake)

- **Premise:** You, walking back alone across a frozen Minnesota lake in early December after ice
  fishing. Near the creek inlet the ice is only **6 cm** thick. It cracks and you go in. Clock:
  **1 minute** to get your breathing back, **10 minutes** of useful strength, about **1 hour**
  before you pass out. Resource: your arms, your boots, a phone in a zip bag, and your own
  footprints behind you.
- **Twist:** Everyone thinks the cold kills you in seconds, so they thrash and try to climb out
  the far side. You should spend the first minute only on breathing, because you have about ten
  minutes. Then turn back the way you came (that ice already held you), put your arms on it, kick
  until your body is flat, and slide out like a seal. Once you're out, don't stand up. Roll away.
- **Hook:** "Fall through the ice? You have ten minutes, not ten seconds." (11 words)
  - Alt: "If the ice cracks under you, don't stand up." (9)
- **Persistent world:** A huge white lake under a low pink December sun. Ice-fishing shacks in a
  row far away, the hero's footprints trailing behind, a dark patch of thin ice near the inlet,
  and the pine shoreline. A side cutaway shows the water layer under the ice.
- **Diagrams (3 + 1):**
  1. **Ice-thickness ladder:** walking 10 cm, snowmobile 13-18 cm, car 20-30 cm, truck 30-38 cm.
     The hero's ice sits at 6 cm and flashes red.
  2. **1-10-1 timeline bar** that fills in real time: 1 MIN BREATHE, 10 MIN MOVE, 1 HR.
  3. **Side cutaway of the self-rescue:** body vertical, then horizontal and kicking, then sliding
     onto the ice, then rolling away.
  4. (Bonus) Top-down view: the footprints show the "good ice" direction, and an arrow says TURN
     BACK.
- **HUD:** Ice thickness (cm), water temp "≈0 °C", the 1-10-1 phase timer, and "DISTANCE TO SHORE"
  (m).
- **Cliffhanger / Part 2:** The hero is out, rolling, safe. Then comes another crack. A friend
  from the shacks is sprinting toward the hole to help. "Rule four: never run to the hole. And he
  was running." PART 2. The next episode is the rule "reach, throw, don't go".
- **Fact to verify (the reveal):** The **"1-10-1 principle"** of cold-water immersion from
  **Dr. Gordon Giesbrecht, University of Manitoba**: about 1 minute of cold-shock gasping, about 10
  minutes of meaningful movement, and up to about 1 hour before unconsciousness from hypothermia.
  Supporting fact: the **Minnesota DNR ice-thickness guidelines** (4 in ≈ 10 cm of new clear ice
  for walking; 5-7 in snowmobile/ATV; 8-12 in car; 12-15 in truck). Verify the current DNR numbers
  and the advice to "turn toward the direction you came from".
- **Main risk:** Life-safety accuracy, since kids walk on ice. Every step must match DNR and
  Giesbrecht exactly. The script must not suggest one hour means you can relax. Rewarming stays at
  "get warm, call 911", with no medical steps. Originality is good: the topic is rarely animated.
- **Score: 9/9** (a3 b3 c3). It's also timely: ice season starts within weeks of today
  (Nov 1, 2026).

## Variant C: "The Sea Walked Away" (tsunami x Cannon Beach, Oregon)

- **Premise:** You, on a sunny Saturday at Cannon Beach, Oregon, near Haystack Rock. The ground
  just shook hard for a long time and everyone laughed it off. Now the ocean is pulling back,
  leaving fish on wet sand. The first wave could come in **as little as about 15 minutes** (to
  verify). Resource: your legs, the blue "Tsunami Evacuation Route" signs, and a hill about 1 km
  inland.
- **Twist:** Everyone walks out onto the new seabed to film and pick up shells, or runs to their
  car. You should go up and inland on foot right away, without waiting for a siren. The road jams,
  and the first wave is often not the biggest one.
- **Hook:** "If the sea suddenly walks away from the beach, run uphill." (11 words)
  - Alt: "The ocean just vanished. Don't go look. Go up." (9)
- **Persistent world:** A bright Oregon beach with the big sea stack, tide pools, beach towels,
  kites, a small town street climbing a forested hill, and blue evacuation signs on poles. The
  shoreline visibly retreats, and the light shifts from sunny to an eerie flat grey.
- **Diagrams (3 + 1):**
  1. Sea-floor cross-section: the trough arrives first so the water pulls back, then the crest
     follows ("THE PULL-BACK IS THE WARNING").
  2. Map: beach, town, hill. A red jammed road with cars sits next to a green walking route that
     follows the signs to the "ASSEMBLY AREA".
  3. Wave-speed diagram: in deep ocean it moves "as fast as a jet" and stays low. Near shore it
     slows and grows tall.
  4. (Bonus) A wave series with the 2nd and 3rd bars taller than the 1st: "THE FIRST ISN'T THE
     BIGGEST".
- **HUD:** "SINCE SHAKING 00:03:40" timer, the hero's elevation (m), distance to high ground (km),
  and a shoreline-retreat meter (m).
- **Cliffhanger / Part 2:** From the hilltop, the first wave has come and gone. On the road below,
  a man is walking back down toward the beach to get his car. "The second wave was already
  coming." PART 2.
- **Fact to verify (the reveal):** **Tilly Smith**, a 10-year-old British girl on **Maikhao Beach,
  Phuket, Thailand, 26 December 2004**, recognised the receding sea from a geography lesson and
  warned her parents. The beach was cleared, and reports say about 100 people were saved. Verify
  the beach name, her age and the number saved. Secondary claim to check: Oregon official guidance
  that a local Cascadia tsunami could arrive in **about 15-20 minutes** and that you should
  evacuate on foot.
- **Main risk:** Not every tsunami starts with the sea pulling back, so the script must also say
  "strong long shaking = go now". The 2004 death toll is heavy, so mention Tilly only as a hero
  story and don't dwell on the disaster. The "receding sea" twist is partly known.
- **Score: 8/9** (a3 b2 c3)

## Variant D: "Floor 47" (stuck elevator x Chicago blackout)

- **Premise:** You, alone in a glass-walled office tower in downtown Chicago. A citywide blackout
  stops the elevator **between floors 47 and 48**. It's dark except the emergency light. Your phone
  is at **9%**. Clock: phone battery and your nerve. Resource: the alarm button, the intercom, the
  emergency light, and patience.
- **Twist:** Everyone thinks the cable will snap or the air will run out, so they pry the doors
  open and climb out. You should stay put. The car can't free-fall (several cables plus automatic
  safety brakes), it isn't airtight, and the open shaft is the real danger. Press the alarm, use
  the intercom, save your battery, and wait for the fire department.
- **Hook:** "Stuck in an elevator at floor 47? Don't touch the doors." (11 words)
  - Alt: "The elevator stopped between floors. The doors are the danger." (10)
- **Persistent world:** A cutaway of the whole tower against the Chicago skyline at dusk, with the
  river and its bridges and the city going dark block by block. The camera dives down the shaft to
  the single car with the hero inside, and the counterweight hangs alongside.
- **Diagrams (3 + 1):**
  1. Shaft cutaway: several hoist cables, a counterweight, and safety brakes gripping the rails,
     labeled "IT CAN'T FREE-FALL".
  2. Between-floors diagram: the doors open onto a wall and a gap down the open shaft, with a red
     X on "CLIMB OUT".
  3. Air-flow diagram: arrows pass through gaps around the doors and the vents, with a gauge
     reading "AIR: FINE".
  4. (Bonus) Phone battery strategy: brightness down, one call, then airplane mode.
- **HUD:** Floor indicator "47½", minutes stuck, phone battery %, and an "AIR" meter that never
  moves, used as a running gag.
- **Cliffhanger / Part 2:** The doors are forced open from outside, but it isn't the fire
  department. A stranger's hand reaches down through the gap: "Jump, I'll catch you." The rule
  says no. PART 2.
- **Fact to verify (the reveal):** In **1854**, at the **Crystal Palace exhibition in New York**,
  inventor **Elisha Otis** stood on a raised elevator platform and had its only hoisting rope cut,
  and his safety brake stopped the fall. (Brand risk: the name is also a company today. Say "an
  inventor" on screen and keep the name only in sources.) Alt fact: **Betty Lou Oliver** survived
  a 75-storey elevator fall in the **Empire State Building on July 28, 1945**, after a B-25 bomber
  hit the building and cut the cables. It's a Guinness record. But it's NYC again, so keep it as a
  one-line aside only.
- **Main risk:** It's a weaker "disaster" with lower stakes, and the twist is semi-known. Blackout
  overlap is mild because it happens in a different space. Safety: there must be nothing about
  electrical panels or the machine room.
- **Score: 7/9** (a3 b2 c2)

## Variant E: "Stay in the Car" (wildfire x California canyon road)

- **Premise:** You, driving down a narrow two-lane canyon road in the California foothills while
  evacuating late. The wind shifts and fire jumps the road ahead. You can't turn around. Clock: the
  **fire front passes in minutes**. Resource: the car, a wool blanket in the trunk, and a water
  bottle.
- **Twist:** Everyone jumps out and runs, because they're afraid the gas tank will explode. You
  should stay in the car. Park away from the heaviest brush, keep the headlights on and the
  ignition off, close the windows and vents, get on the floor under the blanket, and stay until
  the front passes. Metal tanks rarely explode, and the car shields you from heat. Rule zero is
  still to leave early, so this is the last-resort plan.
- **Hook:** "Fire crosses the road ahead? Don't run. Stay in the car." (11 words)
  - Alt: "Trapped by wildfire in your car? Your car is the shelter." (10)
- **Persistent world:** A winding canyon road at golden hour, with dry chaparral hills, a ridge
  of orange glow, and ember particles (three.js). A cutaway of the car interior shows the hero on
  the floor. The light goes from gold to deep orange to smoky blue after the front passes.
- **Diagrams (3 + 1):**
  1. Road choice from above: "HEAVY BRUSH" zones in red and a "BARE PULLOUT" in green, where the
     hero parks.
  2. Car cutaway checklist: windows up, vents closed, lights on, ignition off, hero on the floor
     under the blanket.
  3. Fire-front timeline: a moving band labeled "FRONT ARRIVES · PASSES · SAFE TO MOVE", with the
     car as the shield and radiant-heat arrows bouncing off.
  4. (Bonus) Myth card: "GAS TANK EXPLODES?" with a "RARELY" stamp.
- **HUD:** Distance to fire front (m), wind direction arrow, a "FRONT PASSING" phase timer, and a
  cabin "SMOKE" meter that rises a little, then clears. No invented temperatures.
- **Cliffhanger / Part 2:** The front passes, and the hero sits up into smoky blue light. Through
  the haze, a figure is walking on foot down the road toward the next ridge of fire. "He got out of
  his car." PART 2.
- **Fact to verify (the reveal):** CAL FIRE "Ready, Set, Go!" guidance on surviving in a vehicle
  (readyforwildfire.org): park away from heavy vegetation, headlights on, ignition off, windows up
  and vents closed, get on the floor and cover up with a blanket or coat, stay in the vehicle until
  the main fire passes, "Stay in the car. Do not run!", and **"Metal gas tanks and containers
  rarely explode."** Verify the exact current wording.
- **Main risk:** Sensitivity. People died in cars in the 2018 Camp Fire, and the January 2025 LA
  fires are recent, so the episode could read as exploiting tragedy. The episode must frame this as
  a last resort after "leave early". Fire and fear content risks platform limits. Keep it calm with
  no burning people.
- **Score: 7/9** (a3 b3 c2, minus 1 for sensitivity)

**Recommendation: B, "Ten Minutes Under."** It uses none of "14 Minutes" (open white lake vs dark
basement, solo vs crowd, cold vs fallout, footprints shot vs phone-alert shot). Its three numbers
(1-10-1) and the ice-thickness ladder give a counter that moves in every line. The safety advice
is well established (Minnesota DNR plus Giesbrecht) and the twist makes viewers calmer instead of
scaring them. Publishing in December matches the season. Put A on the calendar for spring as the
next episode.
